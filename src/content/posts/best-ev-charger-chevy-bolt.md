The Chevy Bolt is the rare EV where the right home charger is almost entirely determined by one number: the model year. Buy the wrong amperage and you either pay for speed the car cannot use, or leave the car's full charging rate on the table. The good news is that the Bolt is also one of the easiest EVs to shop for — every 2017–2023 Bolt uses the standard J1772 plug, no adapter is needed, and even a modest charger fills the battery overnight. This guide sorts the model-year split, the right amperage for each Bolt, and the specific chargers worth buying, with every specification taken from verified model data rather than marketing claims.

## The Bolt's charging limit, by model year

Every EV has an onboard charger — the AC-to-DC converter built into the car — and that component, not the wall charger, sets the maximum home charging speed. The wall charger can offer more power than the car accepts, but the car will only draw what its onboard charger allows. For the Bolt, that limit changed once, and the changeover year is the whole buying decision:

| Bolt model years | Onboard charger | Max AC rate | Battery (approx.) | EPA range |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2017–2021 Bolt EV | 7.2 kW | 32 A | 60 kWh (2017–2019), 66 kWh (2020–2021) | Up to 259 miles |
| 2022–2023 Bolt EV | Up to 11.5 kW | 48 A | 65 kWh | 259 miles |
| 2022–2023 Bolt EUV | Up to 11.5 kW | 48 A | 65 kWh | 247 miles |

Chevrolet's own 2022 press material lists the 11.5 kW onboard charging module as new for 2022, alongside the dual-level charge cord with changeable 120-volt and 240-volt plugs. One nuance is worth stating plainly: contemporary reporting at the time described the 11.5 kW charger as optional on the 2022 Bolt EV, standard on the Bolt EUV, and standard across the 2023 lineup. If you own a 2022 Bolt EV and are unsure which onboard charger it has, check the window sticker or the owner's manual before buying — the difference between 32 and 48 amps is the difference between a $300 charger and a $600 one, and guessing wrong in either direction wastes money. For 2023 models and for every Bolt EUV, 11.5 kW is the safe assumption.

The other numbers in the table matter for expectations, not for charger choice. The Bolt EV's 259-mile EPA range and the EUV's 247-mile rating both come from a battery of roughly 65–66 kWh in the later cars. That is a mid-size battery by current standards — big enough that Level 1 charging on a household outlet is genuinely slow (Chevrolet estimates about 4 miles of range per hour), and small enough that any Level 2 charger fills it overnight. The Bolt's DC fast-charging rate, up to about 55 kW on 2017–2023 cars, is a separate matter for road trips and has no bearing on home charger choice: no home Level 2 charger uses the DC pins.

A final model-year note: Chevrolet is bringing the Bolt back as a redesigned 2027 model with a 65 kWh lithium-iron-phosphate battery, a native NACS charge port on the driver-side front fender, and up to 150 kW DC fast charging. That car is a different buying decision — a NACS charger, or a dual-connector charger, rather than the J1772 picks below — and this guide focuses on the 2017–2023 Bolts that make up the overwhelming majority of Bolts on the road today. If you are buying ahead for a 2027 Bolt, the [NACS versus J1772 explainer](/posts/nacs-vs-j1772-explained/) covers the connector change, and the Tesla Universal Wall Connector review on this site covers a charger that handles both connector types.

## J1772 native fit: no adapter, no fuss

For a 2017–2023 Bolt, home charging could not be simpler on the connector front. The Bolt's charge port is a SAE J1772 / CCS Combo port: the round J1772 section on top handles AC charging at home and at public Level 2 stations, and the two larger pins below it handle DC fast charging. Every J1772 home charger plugs straight in — no adapter, no setting, no compatibility check. The port sits on the driver-side front fender, which also makes the cable-length math easy: mount the charger on the garage wall on the driver's side of the car's usual parking spot and even a 24 ft cable has room to spare. Our [cable-length guide](/posts/ev-charger-cable-length-guide/) works through the measurement for trickier layouts, including two-car garages and driveway charging.

That native fit is also why Bolt owners should be wary of one specific purchase: a NACS-only charger. Tesla's Wall Connector (Gen 3) is an excellent charger, but it is NACS-only, so a Bolt owner would need a separate NACS-to-J1772 adapter to use it at home — an extra purchase, an extra connection, and a needless complication when J1772 chargers of equal quality exist at every price point. The exception is a household that will soon add a Tesla or another NACS-native EV, in which case a dual-connector charger such as the Tesla Universal Wall Connector, with its integrated J1772 adapter nested in the handle, earns its premium. For a Bolt-only garage, buy J1772 and spend the difference on the install.

The reverse mistake is buying a charger for DC fast charging at home. There is no home DC fast charger for a Bolt in any practical sense — DC charging hardware costs thousands, needs commercial-grade electrical service, and the Bolt's 55 kW DC rate would not justify it. Home charging for a Bolt means Level 2 AC charging, full stop. If a listing promises "fast charging" for home use, it means a 40–48 amp Level 2 charger, and the numbers in this guide are the honest version of that claim.

## Right-sizing: 32 amps, 40 amps, or 48 amps?

Here is the question every Bolt owner actually faces, answered by model year.

### 2017–2021 Bolt: 32 amps is the ceiling

A pre-2022 Bolt draws at most 32 amps, or 7.2 kW. Plug it into a 48-amp charger and it charges at 32 amps. Plug it into a 50-amp charger and it still charges at 32 amps. The extra amperage is not harmful — the car and the charger negotiate the rate, and the car simply takes what it can — but it buys nothing. A 32-amp charger fills the roughly 60–66 kWh battery in about 9 hours from empty, and adds roughly 25–28 miles of range per hour depending on efficiency. Since most owners charge overnight and rarely arrive home near empty, that is more than enough for daily driving: an 8-hour overnight session at 32 amps adds around 200 miles of range, which covers several days of average driving.

Does that mean a pre-2022 Bolt owner should buy the cheapest 32-amp charger available? Not quite. A 40-amp charger costs little more than a 32-amp one, runs on the same kind of 50-amp circuit, and leaves headroom if the household's next EV accepts more — which most current EVs do. The [Grizzl-E Classic](/chargers/grizzl-e-classic/), at 40 amps with DIP-switch adjustment down to 16 amps, is the classic version of this buy: it charges a pre-2022 Bolt at the car's full rate, it can be dialed down for a smaller circuit, and it will charge a future EV faster without being replaced. Buying a 48-amp charger purely to future-proof a pre-2022 Bolt is defensible only if the next EV is already planned and the 60-amp circuit is already being installed; otherwise it is paying for a 60-amp circuit and a hardwired install to serve a car that cannot use either.

### 2022–2023 Bolt: 48 amps is the full rate

A 2022–2023 Bolt with the 11.5 kW onboard charger draws up to 48 amps, or 11.5 kW — exactly the output of a 48-amp home charger on a 60-amp circuit. Here, a 48-amp charger is not overkill; it is the matched charger. It fills the roughly 65 kWh battery in about 7 hours from empty and adds up to about 39 miles of range per hour on the Bolt EV (about 37 on the EUV, whose efficiency is slightly lower). A 40-amp charger on the same car delivers 9.6 kW and takes about 7.5–8 hours — a difference of roughly half an hour to an hour on a full charge, and a difference most owners will never notice on an overnight charge. That is why 40 amps remains a legitimate choice even for a 2022–2023 Bolt: it is cheaper, it can plug into a NEMA 14-50 outlet instead of being hardwired, and the overnight result is nearly identical.

A 50-amp charger, such as the ChargePoint Home Flex at its maximum setting, gives a 2022–2023 Bolt nothing over a 48-amp charger. The car's 48-amp ceiling means the extra 2 amps go unused. That does not make a 50-amp charger a bad buy — the Home Flex is a strong smart charger for other reasons, and its adjustable output lets you set it to 48 amps for the Bolt — but do not pay extra for the 50-amp headline number on a Bolt's behalf. And an 80-amp charger is pure waste for any Bolt: no Bolt accepts anything near 80 amps, and the 100-amp circuit it needs is a major electrical project for zero benefit.

### The overnight math for every Bolt

The table below uses the charge times reported for the Bolt's battery sizes at each amperage, rounded for readability. Real times vary with battery temperature, starting charge level, and charging losses of roughly 10%.

| Charger setting | Power at 240 V | Full-charge time, 2022–2023 Bolt | Full-charge time, 2017–2021 Bolt |
|---|---|---|---|
| 32 A | 7.7 kW | ~8.5 hours (car caps at 32 A only on pre-2022; a 2022+ car charges at the charger's 32 A setting) | ~9 hours |
| 40 A | 9.6 kW | ~7.5–8 hours | ~9 hours (car caps at 32 A) |
| 48 A | 11.5 kW | ~7 hours | ~9 hours (car caps at 32 A) |

Read the table as a statement about overnight charging, not about speed records. The difference between the slowest and fastest row is about two hours on a completely empty battery — a battery state most owners almost never see, since the Bolt is typically topped up from 40–60% overnight. Our [charging-speeds guide](/posts/ev-charger-charging-speeds-miles-per-hour/) generalizes the same math to other EVs, and the [charge-time calculator](/tools/charge-time-calculator/) lets you plug in your own daily mileage and starting charge to see what your Bolt actually needs. For most Bolt owners, the honest answer is that 32 amps is enough, 40 amps is comfortable, and 48 amps is the matched maximum — and the choice between them should be driven by the circuit you already have and the next EV you might buy, not by the headline number.

## The picks: value first, matched to the car

Every pick below is a J1772 charger with specifications verified from this site's model data. Prices are typical street-price ranges as of this writing — check the current price before buying, since charger prices move with sales and seasons.

### Best value for any Bolt: Grizzl-E Classic

The [Grizzl-E Classic](/chargers/grizzl-e-classic/) is the value champion for Bolt owners, and the reasons map directly onto the Bolt's needs. It delivers up to 40 amps (9.6 kW) through a 24 ft J1772 cable, plugs into a NEMA 14-50 outlet so no hardwiring is required, and costs roughly $300–$450. Its amperage adjusts with DIP switches from 16 to 40 amps, which means it can be matched to a 20-amp, 30-amp, 40-amp, or 50-amp circuit without an app — useful in an older garage where the available circuit is smaller than ideal. The cast-aluminum NEMA 4 enclosure is built for outdoor and harsh-weather use, and the 3-year warranty is standard for the class. The trade-offs are deliberate: no Wi-Fi, no app, no scheduling from the charger. For a Bolt owner, that matters less than it sounds, because the Bolt itself handles scheduled charging through the car's own settings and the myChevrolet app — the charger just needs to deliver power when the car asks for it. Buy the Classic if you want the toughest box for the least money and do not need an app. Check that you are buying the 24 ft variant, since an 18 ft version of the Classic is also sold.

### Best 48-amp match for a 2022–2023 Bolt: Emporia Pro

The [Emporia Pro](/chargers/emporia-pro/) is the pick for a 2022–2023 Bolt owner who wants the car's full 11.5 kW rate. It delivers 48 amps (11.5 kW) when hardwired on a 60-amp circuit, or 40 amps (9.6 kW) on a NEMA 14-50 plug, through a 25 ft J1772 cable — the joint-longest cable in our catalog, which suits the Bolt's front-fender charge port in almost any garage layout. Its headline feature is PowerSmart dynamic load management: paired with Emporia's Vue 3 home energy monitor, it can charge at a full 48 amps on a panel that otherwise could not support a 60-amp circuit, by backing off when the house load rises. That can save thousands on a panel upgrade in an older home, which is where the Pro earns its ~$599 price against cheaper 48-amp options. The Emporia app adds scheduling, real-time energy tracking, and solar-charging integration. The trade-offs: $599 is not the cheapest 48-amp smart charger, and the load-management bundle adds install complexity. Buy the Pro if you have a 2022–2023 Bolt, a tight panel, or both. The [ChargePoint Home Flex](/chargers/chargepoint-home-flex/) is the premium smart-charger pick, and the Bolt-relevant version is the J1772 one — ChargePoint also sells a native-NACS version, so confirm the connector on the listing before buying. Typical street price is roughly $500–$600, with deal prices lower. For a Bolt, set the Home Flex to 48 amps (or 40 amps on a plug-in install) and it behaves exactly like a matched 48-amp charger; the extra 2 amps at its maximum setting go unused by the car. Buy the Home Flex if you road-trip on the ChargePoint public network and want one app for home and road, or if you value the most polished app in the class over the lowest price.

### Best feature-dense pick: Autel MaxiCharger AC Home

The [Autel MaxiCharger AC Home](/chargers/autel-maxicharger-ac-home/) packs the densest connectivity and smart-charging stack of any charger in our catalog: Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and Ethernet, an app with off-peak and time-of-use optimization, energy tracking, over-the-air updates, and dynamic load balancing. It delivers up to 50 amps (12 kW) hardwired or 40 amps (9.6 kW) on a NEMA 14-50 plug, through a 25 ft J1772 cable, at roughly $399–$599 depending on the version. For a Bolt owner, the honest assessment is that most of that capability is surplus: the Bolt's own scheduling covers off-peak charging, and the car caps the rate at 48 amps regardless. Buy the Autel if you want the most capable smart charger and a future-proof install for a faster-charging next EV, not because the Bolt needs any of it today. A NACS variant exists for the 50-amp hardwired model, so confirm the J1772 version on the listing.

The [EVIQO Gen 2](/chargers/eviqo-gen2/) is the sleeper value pick for a 2022–2023 Bolt owner who wants the full 48-amp rate without the premium price: 48 amps (11.5 kW) hardwired, 40 amps on a NEMA 14-50 plug, a 25 ft J1772 cable, Wi-Fi app control with scheduling and energy tracking, and a NEMA 4 / IP66 enclosure — the highest weather rating in our roundup — at roughly $379–$429 street price, well below its $514 list price. The app draws mixed reviews, and EVIQO is a smaller brand with a shorter track record than ChargePoint or Grizzl-E, but the hardware value is hard to argue with. Buy the EVIQO if the budget matters more than the brand name and you are comfortable with a less polished app. As with every pick here, verify the J1772 version and the current street price before buying.

### What to skip

- **An 80-amp charger.** No Bolt accepts more than 48 amps. An 80-amp charger needs a 100-amp circuit, costs substantially more, and delivers exactly the same charging speed to a Bolt as a 48-amp charger. Skip it unless the household's next EV is a large truck with dual onboard chargers.
- **A NACS-only charger for a 2017–2023 Bolt.** A NACS-only unit such as the Tesla Wall Connector Gen 3 needs a separate adapter for a Bolt, for no benefit. Buy J1772, or buy a dual-connector unit if a NACS car is coming.
- **A charger with a cable that cannot reach.** The [Lectron V-BOX Pro](/chargers/lectron-vbox-pro/) is a legitimate budget 48-amp charger at roughly $350–$410, but its 16 ft cable is the shortest in our catalog. It is a fine pick when the charger mounts within a few feet of the Bolt's charge port and a poor one otherwise. Measure before buying any short-cable charger; the cable-length guide linked above shows how.
- **Paying extra for the 50-amp headline.** A 50-amp charger charges a Bolt no faster than a 48-amp one. Buy a 50-amp charger for its app, its install flexibility, or its future-proofing — never for the number alone.
- **Unlisted, no-name chargers.** The Bolt's charging circuit runs for hours at high continuous current. Buy a UL-listed charger from an established brand, on a properly sized circuit, installed to code. A bargain charger without a listing is not a bargain.

## Install: hardwired or plug-in, and what circuit?

The Bolt decision and the install decision are linked, because the circuit determines the maximum amperage and the install type determines whether you need an electrician.

A 40-amp charger on a NEMA 14-50 plug needs a dedicated 50-amp circuit, per the electrical code's 80% rule for continuous loads: a circuit can carry a continuous load of only 80% of its rating, so 40 amps of charging needs a 50-amp breaker. A 48-amp charger needs a dedicated 60-amp circuit and must be hardwired — plug-in NEMA 14-50 outlets top out at 40 amps of charging. That is the practical core of the choice: plug-in 40-amp charging is the simpler, cheaper install (an electrician still installs the outlet and circuit, but the charger itself plugs in and can move with you), while hardwired 48-amp charging is the full-rate install for a 2022–2023 Bolt and needs an electrician for the charger as well. Our [breaker-size guide](/posts/what-size-breaker-for-ev-charger/) covers the 80% rule and every common amperage, and the [panel capacity checker](/tools/panel-capacity-checker/) estimates whether your panel has room before you call an electrician.

Two install cautions apply to every Bolt garage. First, have a licensed electrician do the circuit work, and have them verify the outlet and wiring if you are reusing an existing 240-volt outlet — older dryer outlets and repurposed range outlets are not all rated for hours of EV charging at full current. Second, check for rebates before you buy: many utilities offer rebates on the charger, the install, or both, and some require a Wi-Fi-connected charger or enrollment in a managed-charging program, which can flip the choice between a dumb charger such as the Grizzl-E Classic and a smart one such as the Emporia Pro or ChargePoint Home Flex. Our [installation cost and rebates guide](/posts/level-2-ev-charger-installation-cost-rebates/) covers the cost ranges and where to look.

If you rent, or if a panel upgrade is off the table, the Bolt is forgiving: a 32-amp charger on a 40-amp circuit, or even the dual-level charge cord Chevrolet supplied with 2022–2023 Bolts on a properly installed 240-volt outlet, fills the car overnight. Our [portable charger guide for renters](/posts/best-portable-level-2-ev-charger-renters/) covers the no-permanent-install options. And if you are still deciding between charger models generally, the [best Level 2 chargers roundup](/posts/best-level-2-ev-chargers-2026/) and the [guide to choosing a Level 2 charger](/posts/how-to-choose-a-level-2-ev-charger/) put the Bolt picks in the wider context.

## Bottom line

Match the charger to the model year, not to the biggest number on the box. A 2017–2021 Bolt needs nothing more than a 32–40 amp J1772 charger — the Grizzl-E Classic is the value pick, and a 48-amp charger buys that car nothing. A 2022–2023 Bolt can use the full 48 amps, so a 48-amp charger such as the Emporia Pro or EVIQO Gen 2 is the matched buy, with a 40-amp plug-in charger a close and cheaper alternative that still fills the car overnight. Every Bolt from 2017 to 2023 plugs in natively with J1772, so skip the adapters, skip the 80-amp units, and spend the money you save on a proper circuit and a charger mounted where its cable reaches the front-fender charge port without a stretch. Check your model year's onboard charger in the manual if you own a 2022 Bolt EV, check the current price before buying, and let the overnight math — not the headline amperage — make the final call.

## Frequently asked questions
