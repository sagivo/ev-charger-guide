No garage? No problem — plenty of EV owners charge outdoors every day, in rain, snow, and blazing sun. The US Department of Energy confirms that outdoor home charging is safe, *including in the rain*, as long as you use outdoor-rated equipment installed to code. The catch: not every charger is actually built for it. An indoor-rated box on a driveway wall will fail eventually — and failure means corroded contacts, dead electronics, or a charger that stops working the winter you need it most.

This guide covers what "outdoor-rated" really means (NEMA 4 vs. 4X, IP66 vs. IP67), the weatherproof chargers with the strongest ratings and real owner track records, and the install details that matter as much as the hardware.

## What the ratings actually mean

Enclosure ratings describe what the box can withstand — dust, water, ice — under standardized tests. For outdoor EV chargers, you want one of these:

- **NEMA 4:** indoor or outdoor protection against falling dirt, rain, sleet, snow, windblown dust, splashing and hose-directed water, and ice formation on the enclosure. This is the baseline for a truly watertight home charger.
- **NEMA 4X:** everything in NEMA 4, plus corrosion resistance. If you live near the coast or in a road-salt-heavy climate, this is the rating to look for.
- **NEMA 3R:** outdoor rain protection only — rain, sleet, snow, and ice formation. Adequate, but no windblown-dust or hose-down protection. Fine under a carport or porch overhang; not the best choice for a fully exposed wall.
- **IP66:** dust-tight enclosure protected against powerful water jets from all directions — roughly equivalent to NEMA 4.
- **IP67:** dust-tight plus protection against temporary immersion (up to 1 meter for 30 minutes). The Grizzl-E line's IP67 rating is why it gets driven-over anecdotes and still works.

One warning from NEMA's own guidance: NEMA and IP ratings aren't directly interchangeable (NEMA also evaluates icing and corrosion), and manufacturers may self-declare conformity. Prefer chargers whose listings come from recognized test labs — UL, ETL, CSA — since the whole unit needs to be safety-certified, not just the box. ## Install details that matter more than the rating

The rating protects the box — but the install protects everything else. These are the details worth getting right:

**Location.** Mount the charger so it's out of direct roof runoff and above where water pools. The enclosure handles rain; it does not handle standing water. The connector holster should sit roughly 3–4 feet off the ground: above splash range and snow-drift level, but comfortable to reach.

**Cable management.** Keep the cable off the ground with a holster or hook. A cable lying in a puddle will wick moisture into the connector over time, and a snowblower will find it. The picks above have 23–25-foot cables — plenty of reach without stretching.

**Cold-weather cable stiffness.** EV cables get stiff in extreme cold. The Grizzl-E Ultimate's cable is known to take a moment to loosen up after a deep freeze; ChargePoint's is notably cold-resistant. If you live where it hits −20 °F, this matters more than you'd think for daily handling.

**Wi-Fi reach.** Smart features (scheduling, energy tracking) need the charger to reach your network. The Autel's Ethernet and Bluetooth options are a real advantage for far-flung driveways. If your router is three walls away from the charger location, test the signal before you commit to a Wi-Fi-only unit.

**The circuit.** Outdoor doesn't change the electrical math — a 48-amp charger still needs a 60-amp circuit (the 80% continuous-load rule) — but it changes the materials: weatherproof conduit, an outdoor-rated disconnect where local code requires one, and GFCI protection. Our [installation cost guide](/posts/level-2-ev-charger-installation-cost-rebates/) covers typical pricing and rebates; the [breaker-size guide](/posts/what-size-breaker-for-ev-charger/) covers the circuit math.

**Plug-in vs. hardwired outdoors.** Hardwired is the safer outdoor choice — no receptacle exposed to weather, no plug to loosen over years of freeze-thaw. If you go plug-in (NEMA 14-50), the receptacle itself must be outdoor-rated and weatherproof, and it must be on a GFCI-protected circuit. Our [hardwired vs. plug-in comparison](/posts/hardwired-vs-plug-in-ev-charger/) goes deeper.

**Coastal corrosion.** Within a few miles of salt water, prioritize NEMA 4X (Grizzl-E Ultimate) over NEMA 4, and check the warranty's fine print for corrosion exclusions. Mounting hardware matters too — stainless fasteners, not the zinc screws in the parts bag.

## What to avoid

- **Indoor-only chargers (NEMA 1/12 or no stated rating)** mounted permanently outdoors. A porch eave helps, but the box isn't sealed for it.
- **No-name Amazon chargers** with impressive-sounding specs and no recognized safety listing (UL/ETL/CSA). The rating on the box means nothing if the whole unit was never independently tested.
- **Mounting where water pools** — against a wall at the bottom of a slope, under a downspout, or in a driveway that floods. The NEMA tests assume the enclosure is mounted sensibly.

## Bottom line

For most outdoor installs, the decision is simple: get a NEMA 4 (or better) charger from an established, safety-certified brand, and put your effort into the mounting location and cable routing. The Grizzl-E Ultimate 48A is the toughest option if the budget stretches; the EVIQO Gen 2 gives you the best weather sealing per dollar; the Grizzl-E Classic is the budget tank; and the Autel MaxiCharger AC Home is the pick for the smartest outdoor setup. Any of the four will charge through weather that keeps you inside — which is exactly the point.
