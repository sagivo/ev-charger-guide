Choosing a Level 2 charger comes down to five decisions, in order: your panel, your amperage, your connector, your install type, and how smart it needs to be. Get those right and the brand almost picks itself. Get them wrong and you'll either overpay for a panel upgrade or buy a charger that doesn't fit your cars.

## 1. Start with your panel, not the charger

Your electrical panel decides everything. A 48-amp charger needs a dedicated 60-amp breaker (continuous loads are capped at 80% of breaker rating); 40 amps needs a 50-amp breaker; a 100-amp service panel in an older home may not have room for either. Before you shop, run our [panel capacity checker](/tools/panel-capacity-checker/) — two minutes of questions, and it tells you what your service can actually spare.

If the answer is "not much," you have two moves: a panel upgrade ($1,500–$3,000, the budget-killer in our [installation cost guide](/posts/level-2-ev-charger-installation-cost-rebates/)), or the [Emporia Pro](/chargers/emporia-pro/) with PowerSmart load management, which can deliver a full 48 A on a panel that otherwise couldn't support it. For many older homes, the Emporia route is thousands cheaper than new service.

## 2. Pick your amperage: 40 vs 48

| Charger output | Miles of range per hour* | Needs |
|---|---|---|
| 32 A / 7.7 kW | ~24 | 40-amp breaker |
| 40 A / 9.6 kW | ~30 | 50-amp breaker |
| 48 A / 11.5 kW | ~37–44 | 60-amp breaker, hardwired |
| 50 A / 12 kW | ~38–45 | 60-amp+ breaker, hardwired |

\*At ~3.2 mi/kWh efficiency; your EV's onboard charger is the final cap — many EVs max out at 48 A or below regardless of what the wall unit offers.

For overnight charging, 40 amps is plenty: a 40-mile day refills in under 90 minutes. Buy 48 if the circuit is cheap to add; don't fund a panel upgrade for the last 8 amps. The one exception is genuinely large battery packs charged on tight schedules — if you regularly arrive near-empty at midnight and leave at 6 AM, the extra ~10 miles/hour matters. Everyone else: model your exact car and circuit in our [charge time calculator](/tools/charge-time-calculator/) before paying for more copper.

## 3. Pick your connector: NACS vs J1772

2026 is the transition year. Teslas and virtually all 2025+ US EVs use NACS natively (formalized as SAE J3400); older non-Tesla EVs use J1772. A home charger is a 10-year appliance, so buy for the cars you'll own across that span, not just today's:

- **NACS-native household:** buy NACS — the [Tesla Wall Connector](amazon:tesla-wall-connector-gen3) (~$440–$475) is the benchmark, or the [ChargePoint Home Flex](amazon:chargepoint-home-flex) NACS variant if you want plug-in flexibility.
- **Older non-Tesla EV you'll keep for years:** J1772 is fine and often cheaper — the [Grizzl-E Classic](/chargers/grizzl-e-classic/) is J1772-only and excellent.
- **Mixed household or undecided next car:** a dual-connector charger like the [Tesla Universal Wall Connector](amazon:tesla-universal-wall-connector) with its integrated Magic Dock, or a J1772 charger plus a ~$50–$70 adapter in the frunk.

## 4. Pick your install: hardwired vs plug-in

Hardwired unlocks 48–50 amps and looks cleanest, but needs an electrician and stays with the house. A NEMA 14-50 plug-in caps at 40 amps but is cheaper to install and moves with you. Renters should go plug-in, period; owners planning to stay should hardwire if the panel allows.

The plug-in caveat that actually matters: the outlet must be industrial grade (Hubbell HBL9450A or Bryant 9450FR, ~$60–$100) with terminals torqued to spec. Cheap dryer outlets are the most common failure point in plug-in installs. Full breakdown: [hardwired vs plug-in](/posts/hardwired-vs-plug-in-ev-charger/).

## 5. Decide how smart it needs to be

Worth paying for: **scheduling** and **energy tracking**. Scheduling is the one feature with a direct payback — where Time-of-Use rates exist, off-peak charging can halve your per-kWh cost, and the savings can cover a smarter charger's premium in months. Energy tracking is genuinely useful for expense reports and for sanity-checking your electric bill.

Nice but optional: solar-charging integration (Emporia does this well), dynamic load management (Emporia Pro, Wallbox Power Boost), multi-unit power sharing (Tesla and Wallbox lead here), voice control. Skip the premium if you'll never open the app — the [Grizzl-E Classic](/chargers/grizzl-e-classic/) exists for exactly this buyer, and its DIP-switch amperage adjustment keeps it code-compliant with zero software.

One more consideration: **cable length.** 24–25 ft covers most garages and all the top picks here offer it. The [Lectron V-BOX Pro](/chargers/lectron-vbox-pro/) is the outlier at 16 ft — a great charger if you park close to the install point, a non-starter if the port is across the garage. Measure first; this is the most common buyer's remorse we see.

## Watch out for

- **Buying amperage your car can't use.** Check your EV's onboard charger limit — many cap at 48 A or below, so a 50 A wall unit buys nothing extra for them.
- **Buying the wrong connector variant.** Several chargers here (ChargePoint, Emporia, Autel, Lectron, EVIQO, Grizzl-E Ultimate) sell separate J1772 and NACS listings. Confirm the connector on the listing before you click buy.
- **Ignoring rebates until after purchase.** Some utility programs require specific models or enrollment before you buy. Our [rebates guide](/posts/level-2-ev-charger-installation-cost-rebates/) covers the gotchas.
- **Forgetting the permit.** Most jurisdictions require one for a new 240 V circuit ($50–$200). An unpermitted install can complicate a home sale or an insurance claim.
- **Skipping the cable measurement.** The [Lectron V-BOX Pro](/chargers/lectron-vbox-pro/) is a great charger with a 16 ft cable — perfect if you park close, useless if the charge port is across a three-car garage. Tape-measure the run before you order anything.
